## Why construction accounting demands a different approach

A restaurant tracks food cost. A dentist tracks patient billing. A general contractor in Washington County juggles multiple active jobs at once, each with its own budget, subcontractor draws, retainage, and change orders, often stretching across two or three tax years. Standard bookkeeping wasn't built for that. It records what happened last month. It doesn't tell you whether a job that's 60% complete has actually collected 60% of its billing, or whether the crew is quietly bleeding margin on materials that ran over budget back in March.

Contractors licensed through the Utah Division of Professional Licensing face bonding requirements, surety reviews, and bank covenants that demand more detail than generalist accounting typically delivers. [Kinexus CPAs & Advisors](/who-we-serve/construction) builds job costing and percentage of completion methods into a contractor's books from day one, not as a scramble before tax time. ## What a WIP schedule tells you about your projects

A work-in-progress schedule is a report comparing what you've billed on a job against what you've actually earned, based on the costs incurred to date. Run one for a single job or roll up your entire active project list, and it answers three questions fast: are you overbilled or underbilled on each contract, what's the true profitability once labor and materials catch up to billing, and how much cash is tied to work that isn't finished yet.

Sureties and bank underwriters often read a contractor's WIP schedule before anything else in the financial package. An underbilled position across several jobs can signal a cash crunch even when the bank balance looks fine. An overbilled position can mask a job that's headed toward a loss. [Shawn T. Gubler, CPA, CTC, MAcc,](/who-we-are/team) and the construction team at Kinexus prepare WIP schedules formatted the way bonding companies and Utah community banks expect to see them, so a request for updated financials doesn't turn into a fire drill.

## How percentage of completion accounting works for your business

The percentage of completion method recognizes revenue and expense as a job progresses instead of waiting until it wraps. The most common calculation is cost-to-cost: costs incurred to date divided by total estimated costs for the job. That ratio gets applied to the contract price to determine revenue earned so far. Get the estimated total cost wrong and every number downstream, WIP position, gross profit, tax liability, is wrong too.

Under IRC Section 460, contractors with long-term contracts are generally required to use percentage of completion for tax purposes once average annual gross receipts cross the threshold set in the tax code, which is indexed for inflation. Smaller contractors often have a choice between percentage of completion, completed contract, or cash-basis reporting, and the right call depends on job size, contract length, and how you want income timed across tax years. GAAP financial statements, the kind a bank or bonding company wants to see, almost always require percentage of completion regardless of which method lands on the tax return. Kinexus CPAs & Advisors keeps both sets of numbers consistent for Utah contractors, so the tax filing and the bonding financials tell the same story.

## Common WIP and job costing mistakes we help contractors fix

Most of the construction bookkeeping problems we inherit share a handful of root causes. We see the same patterns across framing crews, excavation companies, and finish contractors throughout Washington County.

- Estimated total costs that never get updated after the bid, so every WIP calculation downstream runs on stale numbers
- Change orders tracked in email threads instead of the accounting system, understating both revenue and cost on active jobs
- Underbilling that goes unnoticed for months, quietly starving a job of cash while the crew keeps working
- [Retainage](/retainage-is-holding-your-cash-hostage-heres-how-to-plan-around-it) held in one general receivable account instead of tracked job by job, making true cash position impossible to see
- Labor and overhead spread evenly across jobs instead of allocated by actual hours or draws, which distorts profitability on every project in the mix

## Frequently Asked Questions About WIP schedules and percentage of completion accounting

**Q: What is a WIP schedule in construction accounting?**
A: A WIP schedule is a work-in-progress report comparing amounts billed to a customer against revenue earned based on costs incurred so far on a job. **Q: Is percentage of completion accounting required in Utah?**
