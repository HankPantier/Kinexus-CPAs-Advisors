## How entity choice quietly drains business profits

Most business owners pick a structure once, usually in the first year, and never look at it again. A sole proprietorship or single-member LLC felt right when the business was small. Five years and a lot more revenue later, that same structure can be quietly costing thousands of dollars a year.

Here's the mechanism: sole proprietors and default LLCs pay self-employment tax, 15.3%, on every dollar of net profit. An S-corp election lets an owner split income between a reasonable salary and distributions, and distributions aren't subject to that tax. On top of the tax gap, an outdated structure can leave real estate, equipment, or personal assets exposed to business liability that a properly layered entity setup would have shielded.

We see this most often with contractors who started as a one-person LLC and now run crews on multiple job sites, and with service businesses that scaled past six figures without ever revisiting the paperwork filed at formation. The structure that made sense at $80,000 in revenue rarely makes sense at $400,000.

## Signs your current structure no longer fits your business

A structure review isn't something to schedule only when the IRS sends a letter. Certain milestones are reliable signals that the entity on file no longer matches the business actually running.

- Net income has climbed past $60,000 to $80,000 and self-employment tax is now a real line item
- You've brought on a partner, co-owner, or investor and profit-sharing needs formal documentation
- You're operating in more than one location, such as a second restaurant site or a new service territory
- You've purchased rental property or a commercial building and it's titled under your operating business
- You've hired W-2 employees and payroll, workers' comp, and liability exposure have all increased
- You're a physician, dentist, or healthcare provider adding a second practice location or bringing on associates
- You're holding multiple rental properties, flips, or a mix of long-term and short-term rentals under one LLC

Any one of these on its own is worth a conversation. Two or three together usually mean money is being left on the table right now.

## Common restructuring scenarios for St. George business owners

### LLC electing S-corp status
icon: DollarSign

A construction or service-based LLC that's clearing $90,000 or more in net profit often elects S-corp taxation, sets a reasonable owner salary, and takes remaining profit as distributions not subject to self-employment tax. For an owner in that range, the annual tax savings can run into the thousands.

### Real estate investor separating properties
icon: Home

An investor holding four rental properties in a single LLC carries liability risk across all four if one tenant sues. Splitting properties into separate entities, sometimes under a parent holding company, isolates risk property by property. [Real estate investors](/who-we-serve/real-estate-investors) with growing portfolios are the clearest case for this move.

### Entrepreneur scaling into a holding company
icon: Building2

An entrepreneur running two or three related businesses, say a service company and a real estate arm holding the building it operates from, often benefits from a holding structure that centralizes ownership, simplifies financing conversations, and cleans up the balance sheet for a future sale.

## Why timing matters for restructuring decisions

An S-corp election has a filing deadline, generally 75 days into the tax year it's meant to cover. Miss that window and the savings wait another twelve months. The same is true for setting up new entities before a property closing, before a new partner buys in, or before year-end. Structure and entity paperwork filed in January doesn't retroactively apply to income already earned in November.

Waiting until tax season to ask "should my structure be different" almost always means losing a full year of savings that a review in Q3 or Q4 would have captured. Proactive timing is the difference between planning and reacting.
