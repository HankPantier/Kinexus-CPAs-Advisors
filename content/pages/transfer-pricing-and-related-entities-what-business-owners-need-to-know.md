## What transfer pricing means for multi-entity businesses

Transfer pricing sounds like something that only applies to multinational corporations shifting profits between countries. It doesn't. Any time two businesses under common ownership exchange goods, services, rent, or cash, the IRS treats that exchange as a related-party transaction, and the price attached to it has to hold up the same way a deal between strangers would.

That covers more situations than most owners realize. A contractor who runs the operating company through one LLC and holds the equipment or the job site property in another is dealing with transfer pricing. So is a physician whose practice entity pays rent to a separate real estate holding company, or a restaurant group with a management entity charging fees to three locations. The IRS calls this the arm's-length standard: related entities have to price their internal transactions as if they were unrelated parties negotiating in the open market. Get it wrong, even without intent, and the exposure lands on the tax return, not the org chart.

## When related-entity transactions trigger IRS scrutiny

Certain patterns catch attention faster than others. Management fees paid from an operating company to a holding entity are common in construction and multi-location restaurant groups, and examiners look closely at whether the fee reflects real services at a market rate or simply moves income to a lower-taxed entity. Rent charged from a property-holding LLC to the operating business is another frequent flag, especially when the rent doesn't match comparable local lease rates in St George or Washington County.

Intercompany loans raise similar questions. If one entity lends money to a related business without a written note, a market interest rate, and a repayment schedule, the IRS can recharacterize the loan as a disguised distribution or dividend. Real estate investors who move cash between rental entities, and medical practices that split the clinical and real estate sides of the business, run into this constantly. None of these structures are wrong. They just need pricing that a stranger would have agreed to.

## Risks of getting related-entity pricing wrong

When the IRS decides a related-party price wasn't at arm's length, it doesn't just ask for an adjustment. It reallocates income between the entities under IRC Section 482, which can trigger:

- Back taxes on income shifted to the entity that should have reported it
- Accuracy-related penalties on top of the reallocated amount
- Interest that compounds from the original filing date, not the audit date
- A wider audit scope, since one flagged transaction often leads examiners to review every related-entity transfer
- Higher future audit risk once a business has an adjustment on record

The pattern is consistent: the businesses that get flagged usually had a reasonable structure, just no documentation to support it. That gap is what turns a routine notice into a multi-year exam.

## How growing businesses structure multi-entity transactions correctly

Correct transfer pricing starts with documentation, not spreadsheets after the fact. Every intercompany transaction, a management fee, rent, a loan, needs a written agreement, a defensible method for setting the price, and evidence that the method reflects market terms. For rent, that usually means a comparison to lease rates for similar space in the same market. For management fees, it means documenting the actual services performed and benchmarking the fee against what an unrelated provider would charge.

Shawn Gubler, CPA, CTC, MAcc, and Chase Gubler, CPA, MAcc, work with construction groups, real estate investors, and multi-entity medical practices to set up this documentation before the entities start transacting, not after a notice arrives. That often overlaps with [entity structuring](/services/entity-structuring) work: deciding which activities belong in which entity, how they should be capitalized, and how the intercompany agreements between them need to read. Businesses that get this right treat it as infrastructure, the same way they'd treat a chart of accounts or a payroll system.

## Why proactive planning beats after-the-fact fixes

Most transfer pricing problems don't start as tax problems. They start as operational decisions, an owner sets up a second LLC to hold real estate, or splits a practice into a clinical entity and a management entity, and nobody prices the transactions between them until the accountant asks during tax prep. By then the pattern is already on prior returns.

Year-round tax planning catches this before it compounds. Reviewing intercompany transactions as part of ongoing [advanced tax planning](/services/advanced-tax-planning), rather than once during filing season, means pricing gets set with intention and documented in real time. For construction companies with equipment-holding entities, restaurant groups with management companies, and real estate investors running multiple rental LLCs, that difference shows up the first time the IRS asks a question, not the first time a return gets filed.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Transfer pricing and related entities what business owners need to know

**Q: Does transfer pricing apply to small businesses, or only large corporations?**
A: It applies any time related entities under common ownership transact, regardless of size. A contractor with a separate equipment-holding LLC or a physician paying rent to their own real estate entity both fall under the same arm's-length pricing rules the IRS applies to multinational companies.

**Q: What counts as a related-party transaction for tax purposes?**
A: Common examples include management fees between an operating company and a holding entity, rent paid to a property-holding LLC owned by the same person, and intercompany loans between sister businesses. Each needs pricing that matches what an unrelated party would charge or pay.

**Q: What happens if the IRS decides my related-entity pricing wasn't at arm's length?**
A: The IRS can reallocate income under Section 482, assess back taxes on the entity that should have reported it, add accuracy-related penalties, and charge interest from the original filing date. It often widens the audit to review every intercompany transaction, not just the one flagged.

**Q: How do I document intercompany transactions correctly?**
A: Each transaction needs a written agreement, a documented pricing method, and evidence the price reflects market terms, such as comparable lease rates for rent or benchmarked fees for management services. This documentation should exist before the transaction happens, not after a notice arrives.
