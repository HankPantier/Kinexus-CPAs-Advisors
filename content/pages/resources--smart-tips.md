## Smart financial habits that keep St. George businesses ahead

Most CPA firms hand you a checklist every March and go quiet until next tax season. That's not how a growing business stays ahead of cash flow problems, missed deductions, or a bad quarter. The tips below reflect the same proactive, year-round approach Shawn T. Gubler, CPA, CTC, MAcc, and the rest of the Kinexus team use with contractors, restaurant operators, real estate investors, physicians, and service-based businesses across Washington County.

This page is a working resource, not a one-time download. We update it as tax law changes and as the questions we hear most from St. George business owners shift with the seasons. Jump to the section that matches your industry, or read straight through if you're still building your foundation. Either way, treat it as a starting point for a conversation, not a substitute for one.

## Industry-specific tips for construction and contracting businesses

Contractors live and die by job costing, and job costing only works if the numbers are tracked by project, not by month. Retainage sitting on the books for six to twelve months can quietly starve cash flow if it isn't separated from earned revenue. A [WIP schedule](/industries/construction/wip-schedules-percentage-completion) reviewed once a year at bond renewal time is a lagging indicator, not a management tool.

- Track job costs by project so you know which jobs actually make money, not just which ones closed
- Keep retainage receivable separate from regular AR so it stops inflating your cash position
- Review percentage-of-completion and [WIP schedules](/industries/construction/wip-schedules-percentage-completion) quarterly, not annually
- Build change orders into your accounting workflow before the crew leaves the site
- Set aside a fixed percentage of each draw for tax obligations before it hits your operating account

For a deeper look at retainage timing, read [Retainage is holding your cash hostage](/retainage-is-holding-your-cash-hostage-heres-how-to-plan-around-it) or visit our [construction accounting page](/who-we-serve/construction) for the full picture.

## Restaurant and food service accounting best practices

Restaurant margins run thin, usually 3 to 9 percent, which means a bookkeeping error that would barely register in another industry can wipe out a month's profit. Cost of goods sold has to be tracked weekly, not at month's end, because food and beverage prices move fast enough to erase a menu's profitability in a single supplier price hike.

- Reconcile COGS weekly against your POS system, not just at month-end close
- Report tips accurately and on time. Underreporting creates IRS exposure that follows the business, not just the employee
- Separate labor cost by front-of-house and back-of-house to spot overtime creep early
- Forecast cash flow weekly during slow seasons instead of relying on year-end totals
- If you operate more than one location, review your [multi-location franchise tax strategy](/industries/restaurants/multi-location-franchise-tax-strategy) at least annually

More on structuring accounting for multiple locations or franchise growth is on our [restaurant industry page](/who-we-serve/restaurants).

## Tax and cash flow strategies for real estate investors and medical practices

Real estate investors and medical professionals face very different daily operations, but the same tax questions show up on both desks: is the entity structure still doing its job, and is depreciation being planned proactively instead of discovered in April. A cost segregation study can accelerate depreciation on a rental property by reclassifying components into 5, 7, or 15-year schedules instead of the standard 27.5. Physicians and dentists running an S corporation should revisit reasonable compensation annually, since that number drives payroll tax exposure every year it's left unreviewed.

Quarterly estimated payments matter more for both groups than most other client types, because rental income and practice distributions rarely follow a steady monthly pattern. ## Guidance for entrepreneurs, startups, and service-based businesses

Coaches, consultants, agencies, staffing firms, and IT providers usually start with a spreadsheet and a shoebox of receipts. That works for exactly one tax season. Once payroll, contractors, or a second revenue stream enters the picture, the gaps in a DIY system start costing real money in missed deductions and payroll penalties.

- Set up a dedicated business bank account and bookkeeping system before your first invoice, not after your first audit notice
- Choose payroll software or a provider before hiring your first W-2 employee, not the week their first paycheck is due
- Track home office, mileage, and software subscription deductions monthly, since these get missed most often at scale
- Decide on entity structure early. An LLC that made sense at $50,000 in revenue may not at $500,000
- Build a habit of monthly financial reviews instead of an annual scramble

## Year-end checklist and proactive tax planning moves

Year-end tax planning has to happen before December 31, not in April when the options have already closed. Compliance-only firms file what already happened. A proactive review looks for moves that are still available while there's time to act.

- Reconcile bank and credit card accounts through November before your accountant needs clean books
- Review estimated tax payments against actual year-to-date income to avoid an underpayment penalty
- Evaluate retirement plan contributions (SEP IRA, Solo 401(k), or defined benefit plan) while there's still time to fund them
- Consider equipment purchases or Section 179 elections before the calendar closes, not after
- Confirm 1099 vendor information now so January filing isn't a scramble
- Schedule a mid-December planning call instead of waiting for your tax organizer

## Frequently Asked Questions About Smart tips

**Q: What accounting tips should St. George small business owners follow year-round?**
A: Reconcile books monthly, track industry-specific costs like job costing or COGS weekly, review entity structure annually, and make estimated tax payments quarterly. Kinexus CPAs & Advisors recommends monthly financial reviews instead of an annual scramble, catching issues while they're still fixable rather than after filing.

**Q: How is construction bookkeeping different from general small business bookkeeping?**
A: Construction bookkeeping tracks costs by individual job rather than by month, separates retainage from regular receivables, and requires quarterly WIP (work-in-progress) schedule reviews for bonding and cash flow purposes. General small business bookkeeping typically doesn't need this level of project-level detail.

**Q: What's the biggest accounting mistake restaurant owners make?**
A: Waiting until month-end to reconcile cost of goods sold. With margins often between 3 and 9 percent, a supplier price increase or inventory shrinkage discovered a month late can already have erased that period's profit. Weekly COGS reconciliation against POS data catches problems early.

**Q: Do real estate investors and medical practices need the same tax strategy?**
A: No, but both benefit from proactive entity structuring and depreciation planning. Real estate investors often use cost segregation studies to accelerate depreciation, while physicians and dentists in an S corp should review reasonable compensation annually. Both need quarterly, not annual, tax planning.

**Q: When should a startup set up formal bookkeeping and payroll systems?**
A: Before the first invoice or first W-2 hire, not after. Waiting until tax season or a payroll deadline forces reactive decisions on entity structure, deduction tracking, and compliance that are far easier to get right when set up proactively from day one.
