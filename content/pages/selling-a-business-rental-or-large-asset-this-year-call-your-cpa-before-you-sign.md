## Why timing matters before you sign anything

Once a purchase agreement is signed, most of your tax-saving options disappear with it. The sale price is locked, the structure is set, and the IRS treats the transaction as final from that date forward. Business owners across St. George, Washington County, and southern Utah call us every year after they've already accepted an offer, hoping we can soften the tax hit after the fact. Sometimes we can help. Often, the best moves, like restructuring the sale as an asset purchase instead of a stock sale, or timing the closing to land in a lower-income year, were only available before ink hit paper.

Selling a business, a rental property, or a piece of major equipment is one of the biggest financial events a business owner will face. A short conversation with your CPA before you sign preserves options that disappear the moment a deal closes. That's the entire premise behind this page, and it's worth taking seriously before your next big sale.

## Tax consequences of selling a business

How you sell a business changes what you owe almost as much as how much you sell it for. An asset sale and an entity (stock) sale are taxed differently, and buyers and sellers often prefer opposite structures for opposite reasons. Sellers frequently favor a stock sale because gains are taxed at capital gains rates. Buyers usually want an asset sale so they can step up basis and depreciate assets going forward. Whoever negotiates that point without a CPA at the table tends to give up value they didn't know was on the table.

Depreciation recapture is the other piece owners miss. If you've written off equipment, leasehold improvements, or vehicles over the years, part of that gain on sale gets taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains, no matter how the deal is structured. ## What to know before selling a rental or investment property

Real estate investors and landlords have more planning tools available than most sellers realize, but nearly all of them require action before closing.

- **1031 exchanges**, Deferring capital gains by rolling proceeds into another investment property requires a qualified intermediary lined up and a 45-day identification clock that starts at closing, not after.
- **Depreciation recapture**, Every year of depreciation taken on a rental comes back as taxable income at sale, taxed at rates up to 25 percent, regardless of how long you held the property.
- **Installment sales**, Spreading the gain over multiple tax years through seller financing can keep you out of a higher bracket, but the note terms have to be drafted correctly from day one.
- **Cost segregation history**, If you've accelerated depreciation on the property, that acceleration affects both your recapture exposure and your exchange math.

## Large asset sales and equipment disposals

Selling a piece of heavy equipment, a company vehicle, or a medical practice's imaging equipment triggers the same recapture math as selling a building, just on a smaller scale. Contractors who sold a truck or excavator after taking Section 179 or bonus depreciation often owe more in tax on that single sale than they expected, because the deduction they took years ago comes due all at once. Medical professionals selling practice equipment or a service business owner unloading office assets face the identical issue.

The fix usually isn't complicated, but it has to happen before the sale, not on next April's return. Timing the sale to offset against a loss year, pairing it with a new equipment purchase, or spreading multiple asset sales across two tax years are all options while the deal is still being negotiated. Peter Williams, EA, MAcc, and Andy Stewart, EA, regularly walk construction and medical clients through these calculations before assets change hands, using the industry context built through our [construction](/who-we-serve/construction) and [medical professionals](/who-we-serve/medical-professionals) advisory work.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Selling a business rental or large asset this year call your cpa before you sign

**Q: Why should I call a CPA before selling my business or rental property?**
A: Once you sign a sale agreement, the tax structure is largely locked in. A CPA can model asset-vs-entity sale structures, time depreciation recapture, or set up a 1031 exchange only while the deal is still being negotiated, not after closing.

**Q: What is depreciation recapture and how does it affect a sale?**
A: Depreciation recapture taxes the portion of your gain equal to prior depreciation deductions as ordinary income, up to 25 percent on real estate, rather than at lower capital gains rates. It applies to businesses, rentals, vehicles, and equipment alike.

**Q: Can I still do a 1031 exchange after I've already accepted an offer?**
A: Yes, but the window is tight. A qualified intermediary must be arranged before closing, and you have 45 days after the sale to identify replacement property. Waiting until after you sign often makes the exchange impossible.

**Q: Does selling business equipment trigger the same tax issues as selling real estate?**
A: Largely yes. If you took Section 179 or bonus depreciation on equipment, vehicles, or practice assets, selling them can trigger recapture taxed as ordinary income. Timing the sale against income or new purchases can reduce that impact.

**Q: How is Kinexus different from a CPA who only does annual tax prep?**
A: Kinexus runs year-round proactive tax planning rather than once-a-year filing. That means sale, installment, and entity restructuring strategies are already in motion before a business, rental, or asset sale closes, not discovered too late in April.
