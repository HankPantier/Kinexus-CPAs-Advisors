## Why WIP schedules matter more than your monthly P&L

Your profit and loss statement tells you what happened last month. Your work-in-progress schedule tells you what's happening right now, on every job, in real time. For a construction company running five, ten, or twenty active contracts in St. George, that difference matters more than most owners realize.

A P&L rolls up revenue and expenses across the whole business. It can't tell you that Job 214 is bleeding cash while Job 218 is subsidizing it. A properly built WIP schedule can. It breaks performance down contract by contract, showing which jobs are ahead of billing, which are behind on costs, and which need attention before the next draw request goes out.

Contractors who only glance at monthly financials are flying without instruments. The WIP schedule is the instrument panel. Bankers and bonding agents already know this, which is exactly why they ask for it before anyone else does.

## The key numbers your WIP schedule is tracking

A WIP schedule looks intimidating until you know what each column is actually measuring. A handful of numbers drive the whole report, and once you understand them, the schedule stops being a spreadsheet and starts being a diagnostic tool.

- **Contract value**: The total amount you're contracted to earn on the job, including approved change orders.
- **Costs incurred to date**: Every dollar spent on labor, materials, subcontractors, and equipment for that specific job so far.
- **Estimated cost to complete**: What your project manager or estimator believes it will still cost to finish the work. This number is only as good as the person updating it, which is where a lot of schedules go wrong.
- **Percent complete**: Costs incurred divided by total estimated cost, the standard measure under the percentage-of-completion method.
- **Earned revenue**: Percent complete multiplied by contract value, what you should have recognized based on actual progress.
- **Billed to date**: What you've actually invoiced the customer through draws or progress billings.

The gap between earned revenue and billed to date is the over billing or under billing position, and it's the number your banker reads first.

## What over billing and under billing reveal about your projects

Over billing means you've invoiced more than you've actually earned based on progress. It shows up as a liability on your balance sheet, and it often means the cash sitting in your account isn't fully yours yet. That's not always bad. Front-loaded billing on a job with predictable costs can be smart cash management. But chronic over billing across multiple contracts usually signals that owners are billing ahead to cover shortfalls elsewhere, essentially funding one job with another job's cash.

Under billing is the opposite: you've earned more than you've billed. That means you're financing the job out of pocket while the customer holds your cash. A little under billing at the start of a contract is normal. Sustained under billing across several jobs usually points to slow billing cycles, underpriced change orders, or an estimate that was wrong from day one.

Either pattern, tracked project by project, tells you where a job is heading before the final numbers land.

## Why bonding agents and bankers scrutinize this report

Bonding agents and bank underwriters read WIP schedules the way a physician reads bloodwork. They're not focused on your revenue. They're checking whether what you report matches what the underlying numbers actually support.

A surety underwriting a bond wants to see a realistic backlog, over and under billing positions that stay stable from period to period, and minimal gross profit fade, the gap between original estimated margin and current margin. A pattern of unexplained fade across several jobs is one of the fastest ways to lose bonding capacity in Washington County or anywhere else.

Lenders look for the same signals when underwriting a line of credit or an equipment loan. An accurate WIP schedule, updated consistently, supports a higher bonding limit and a stronger borrowing relationship. A sloppy one, thrown together once a year for the CPA, raises questions before a single number is even wrong.

## Common WIP mistakes that hurt contractors financially

Most WIP problems aren't caused by bad math. They're caused by bad habits that compound month after month.

- Outdated cost-to-complete estimates, where a project manager updates the number once and never revisits it as the job changes.
- Inconsistent update cadence, preparing WIP monthly for some jobs and quarterly, or not at all, for others.
- Ignoring change orders until they're fully signed, which understates both revenue and cost in the meantime.
- Treating the WIP schedule as a year-end exercise for the tax return instead of a monthly management tool.
- No reconciliation to the general ledger, so the WIP schedule and the financial statements tell two different stories.

Each one is fixable on its own. Left alone, they compound into a schedule nobody, including the owner, actually trusts.

## Frequently Asked Questions About What a WIP schedule actually tells you and why your banker cares

**Q: What is a WIP schedule in construction accounting?**
A: A work-in-progress schedule tracks billed revenue, earned revenue, costs incurred, and estimated cost to complete on every active job. It shows whether a contractor is over billed or under billed, giving owners, bankers, and bonding agents a real-time view of project profitability rather than a lagging monthly P&L.

**Q: How often should a contractor update their WIP schedule?**
A: Most contractors with several active jobs should update their WIP schedule monthly, not annually. Waiting until tax season or a bond renewal to update cost-to-complete estimates leads to stale numbers that don't reflect job site reality, which undermines credibility with lenders and sureties.

**Q: What does over billing mean on a WIP schedule?**
A: Over billing means you've invoiced a customer for more than you've actually earned based on job progress. It's recorded as a liability and can signal that cash from one job is propping up shortfalls on another, which bonding agents watch closely across multiple contracts.

**Q: Why do bonding companies ask for a WIP schedule?**
A: Sureties use the WIP schedule to judge whether your backlog, billing practices, and profit margins are consistent and realistic. Stable over/under billing positions and minimal gross profit fade support higher bonding capacity, while erratic numbers or unexplained fade can shrink available bond limits.
