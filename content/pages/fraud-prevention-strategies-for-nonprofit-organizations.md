## Why nonprofits face unique fraud risk

A nonprofit runs on trust in a way most businesses don't. Donors assume their gifts go where promised. Grantors assume compliance. Board members, often volunteers with full-time jobs elsewhere, assume the staff handling the books have it covered. That trust is valuable, and it's also exactly what makes nonprofits in St George and across Washington County vulnerable.

Most small nonprofits run lean: one bookkeeper, a part-time volunteer treasurer, or an executive director juggling finance duties alongside programs. When one person controls deposits, disbursements, and reconciliation, there's no second set of eyes to catch a mistake or a deliberate misstatement. That's not a character judgment on your staff. It's a structural gap, and it's the same gap that shows up in embezzlement cases nationwide involving organizations that looked buttoned-up right up until they weren't.

## Core internal controls every nonprofit should implement

You don't need a large finance department to build real fraud protection. You need the right structure around the people you already have. For a small or mid-size nonprofit in Southern Utah, that structure usually comes down to a handful of controls applied consistently.

- Separate the person who approves expenses from the person who pays them
- Require dual signatures or dual approval on disbursements above a set dollar threshold
- Reconcile bank and credit card statements monthly, reviewed by someone outside the transaction chain
- Restrict access to accounting software and online banking to named users, not shared logins
- Rotate or spot-check who prepares deposits versus who records them
- Require original receipts and documented business purpose for every reimbursement
- Review payroll changes, especially new hires and pay rate adjustments, before they run

None of these controls require new headcount. They require a documented process and someone with accounting experience checking that the process is actually followed, month after month, not just when an audit is coming up.

## Bookkeeping and accounting practices that close fraud gaps

Volunteer-managed books feel like a reasonable cost-saving move for a small nonprofit. In practice, they're one of the most common places fraud goes undetected for years. A volunteer treasurer who updates a spreadsheet quarterly, or a part-time bookkeeper without accounting credentials, isn't positioned to catch a duplicate vendor payment or a pattern of small, recurring discrepancies.

Consistent, professionally managed bookkeeping changes that. Monthly reconciliation, coded transactions, and a documented audit trail mean every dollar has a paper trail someone can actually follow. At Kinexus, our bookkeeping team handles routine transaction classification, expense tracking, and reconciliation on a set monthly schedule, so gaps get flagged in weeks, not years. That cadence alone closes most of the windows where undetected discrepancies tend to hide.

## Grant accounting compliance and donor fund oversight

Restricted grant funds carry their own compliance risk, separate from garden-variety fraud. If restricted dollars get commingled with general operating funds, or a grant report misstates how money was spent, the exposure isn't just financial. It's a funder relationship, and for a Utah nonprofit depending on state or foundation grants, that relationship can be the difference between renewal and rejection.

Proper grant accounting means tracking restricted and unrestricted funds separately, documenting how each grant dollar was spent against its approved budget, and producing reports that match what the funder actually requires. This is where generic bookkeeping falls short and nonprofit-specific accounting experience matters. Kinexus works with nonprofit clients to build fund tracking into the chart of accounts from day one, so grant reporting is a byproduct of good recordkeeping, not a scramble every quarter.

## Board and leadership oversight responsibilities

Internal controls only work if someone above the bookkeeping function is actually reviewing the numbers. That's the board's job, and it's one many boards underperform, not from negligence but from not knowing what to ask.

A board finance committee should review a full set of financials, not just a cash balance, on a regular schedule. Members should ask who approved this expense, why this variance exists, and whether bank reconciliations happened this month. Shawn T. Gubler, CPA, CTC, MAcc, and the Kinexus advisory team work directly with nonprofit boards to build a review cadence and a short list of questions that catch problems before they compound. Good governance isn't a compliance checkbox. It's the second layer of defense behind your internal controls.

## How audit protection and outside CPA review add a layer of defense

Even well-run nonprofits benefit from an outside set of eyes. Staff and board members are close to the day-to-day, which is exactly why irregularities can slip past them. An independent CPA review looks at the books with no stake in how the numbers came out, which is a different kind of scrutiny than an internal check.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Fraud prevention strategies for nonprofit organizations

**Q: What is the most common type of fraud at small nonprofits?**
A: Embezzlement through disbursement fraud is most common, typically involving one person controlling both approval and payment of expenses. Separating those duties and requiring dual approval on payments above a set threshold closes the gap most small nonprofits leave open.

**Q: How often should a nonprofit reconcile its bank accounts?**
A: Monthly, at minimum, reviewed by someone who isn't handling daily deposits or payments. Kinexus builds this reconciliation into ongoing bookkeeping service so discrepancies surface within weeks instead of going unnoticed for a full fiscal year.

**Q: Do small nonprofits really need outside CPA review?**
A: Yes. Internal staff and board members are close to daily operations, which is exactly why irregularities can go unnoticed. An independent CPA review, like the audit protection services Kinexus provides, applies outside scrutiny that catches what internal teams often miss.

**Q: What should a nonprofit board ask about finances each quarter?**
A: Boards should review full financial statements, not just cash balances, and ask about unusual variances, who approved specific expenses, and whether monthly reconciliations were completed. Kinexus works directly with nonprofit boards to build this review cadence.

**Q: How does grant accounting relate to fraud prevention?**
A: Restricted grant funds that get commingled with general operating funds create both fraud risk and compliance violations with funders. Proper fund accounting tracks restricted dollars separately and ties spending to approved budgets, protecting both finances and funder relationships.
