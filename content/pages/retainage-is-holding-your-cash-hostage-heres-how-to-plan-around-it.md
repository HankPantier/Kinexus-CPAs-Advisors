## Why retainage puts St George contractors in a cash flow bind

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-right | image: construction-retainage-cash-flow.jpg | alt: "Contractor reviewing pay application paperwork on a Utah job site" | query: "contractor reviewing paperwork construction site" -->

Most Utah construction contracts hold back 5 to 10 percent of every pay application until substantial completion, sometimes longer if punch list items drag on. That money isn't gone. It's just not yours yet, and in the meantime you still owe subs, suppliers, and payroll on the full value of the work you already performed.

For a St George general contractor running $2 million in annual volume, a 7 percent retainage rate can mean $140,000 sitting on someone else's books at any given time. Subcontractors feel this even harder, since they're often waiting on retainage from the GC while the GC is waiting on retainage from the owner. Two layers of delay, one cash flow squeeze.

The contractors who get hurt worst are the ones treating retainage as an afterthought instead of a line item they plan around every month.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image | image: retainage-books-tracking.jpg | alt: "Bookkeeper entering construction job cost data into accounting software" | query: "bookkeeper entering data computer office" -->
## How retainage should be tracked on your books

Retainage isn't a footnote. It needs its own line on the balance sheet, tracked separately from your regular accounts receivable and payable, so your financial statements reflect what's actually collectible now versus what's parked until closeout.

On jobs using percentage-of-completion accounting, retainage still gets recognized as revenue when it's earned, even though the cash hasn't moved. That distinction trips up a lot of contractors who lump retainage in with regular AR and end up with job profitability numbers that don't match reality.

Common mistakes we see on Utah contractor books:

- Retainage buried inside a general "accounts receivable" bucket instead of its own retainage receivable account
- Retainage payable to subs not tracked separately, which understates what you actually owe
- Job cost reports that don't reconcile retainage held against retainage released, hiding margin erosion until it's too late
- WIP schedules built without retainage adjustments, which bankers and bonding agents will catch immediately

Get this wrong and your financials misrepresent both your cash position and your true job margins, which matters the moment a lender or bonding company asks for statements.

## Tax timing traps retainage creates for contractors

Retainage creates a real tax problem: you can end up owing tax on money you haven't collected. Under accrual accounting and percentage-of-completion reporting, retainage is generally recognized as income when earned, not when it's actually paid out. That means a contractor can report six figures of taxable income tied to retainage still sitting with a GC or owner, and still have to write a check to the IRS for it.

Cash-basis contractors face a different version of the same headache. Timing of when retainage is released, and which tax year it lands in, can shift your entire tax liability from one year to the next depending on when a project closes out.

This is exactly where once-a-year tax prep fails contractors. A CPA who only sees your numbers in March can't help you plan for a $300,000 retainage release landing in Q4. Proactive, year-round tax planning means estimating that release, adjusting quarterly payments, and choosing the right accounting method before the surprise hits your bank account instead of after.

## Building a cash flow plan that accounts for held-back funds

A usable cash flow plan for a contractor starts with a retainage schedule: every open contract, the retainage percentage, the expected release date, and the dollar amount. Once that's built, you can see exactly where the gaps are months before they hit.

From there, most St George contractors need a working capital reserve sized to cover payroll and material costs during the stretch between substantial completion and final retainage release, which can run 60 to 120 days depending on the owner and project type. Some also negotiate reduced retainage rates at 50 percent completion, or push for retainage escrow accounts that release faster on public work.

This is where compliance-only accounting falls short. ## Frequently Asked Questions About Retainage is holding your cash hostage heres how to plan around it

**Q: What is retainage in construction accounting?**
A: Retainage is the portion, typically 5 to 10 percent, of each pay application that a contract owner or GC withholds until substantial completion or project closeout. It's earned income but not yet collected cash, which creates tracking and tax timing challenges for contractors.

**Q: How should retainage receivable be recorded on the books?**
A: Retainage receivable should sit in its own account separate from regular accounts receivable, and retainage payable to subcontractors should be tracked the same way. This keeps job profitability and cash position accurate on financial statements and WIP schedules.

**Q: Do contractors pay tax on retainage before they collect it?**
A: Often yes. Under accrual and percentage-of-completion accounting, retainage is generally recognized as taxable income when earned, not when paid. Year-round tax planning helps contractors anticipate this and adjust estimated payments before it becomes a surprise tax bill.

**Q: How can St George contractors manage cash flow with retainage outstanding?**
A: Build a retainage schedule tracking every open contract's release date and amount, maintain a working capital reserve for the 60-120 day gap after completion, and use rolling cash flow forecasts to know when a credit line or reserve draw is needed.

**Q: Why work with a construction-focused CPA instead of a general accountant?**
A: A construction CPA understands job costing, WIP schedules, and retainage timing that generalist firms miss during once-a-year tax prep. Kinexus works with St George contractors year-round on retainage tracking, tax planning, and CFO-level cash flow forecasting.
