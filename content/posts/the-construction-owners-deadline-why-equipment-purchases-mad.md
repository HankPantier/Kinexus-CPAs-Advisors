A St. George excavation contractor we talked with this fall had a skid steer on order since October, paid in full, invoice dated December 28. It didn't roll off the dealer's lot and start moving dirt until January 6. That eight-day gap pushed a $65,000 deduction into a tax year he hadn't planned for, and it cost him real cash the following April. This happens to contractors almost every year, and the mistake is always the same: assuming the date you write the check is the date that counts. It isn't. The IRS cares about when equipment is placed in service, not when you paid for it, and that one distinction is the most avoidable tax leak we see across construction clients in Washington County.

If you run a framing crew, an excavation outfit, an HVAC install business, or a general contracting company bidding commercial jobs from St. George to Cedar City, your equipment purchases are lumpy, expensive, and highly sensitive to timing. A single decision in December can shift tens of thousands of dollars of deduction from one tax year to the next, and that shift matters more than most owners realize until it's too late to fix.

## The date that matters isn't the invoice date

For depreciation purposes, the IRS defines "placed in service" as the date property is ready and available for its assigned function, not the date you ordered it, paid a deposit, or even took title. [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays this out directly: a truck sitting in your yard still wrapped in plastic on December 31 doesn't count. A skid steer that arrived in November but wasn't operational until a hydraulic fitting got replaced in January doesn't count either, at least not for the year you wanted it to.

This rule catches contractors every year for a simple reason: equipment lead times are unpredictable. Dealers quote four weeks and deliver in nine. A specialty attachment gets backordered. A truck needs upfitting before it can go to work. If you're planning to use a large purchase to offset a strong income year, the placed-in-service date is the only date that matters, and it needs to happen on or before December 31 of that tax year, not sometime in the pipeline.

## Bonus depreciation is back at 100 percent, but the deadline discipline still matters

For several years, contractors were racing a shrinking window. Bonus depreciation dropped from 100 percent to 80 percent in 2023, then 60 percent in 2024, then was scheduled to fall to 40 percent for 2025 equipment. Every December brought a real financial reason to buy before the percentage dropped again.

That phase-down has been reversed. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, qualifies for 100 percent bonus depreciation again, and the law makes it permanent rather than another temporary window. That's good news for equipment-heavy contractors, but it doesn't remove the timing pressure, it just changes what you're managing. The deduction is now bigger and more stable, but you still have to hit the placed-in-service deadline to claim it in the year you need it. A contractor with a strong 2025 income year and a truck that doesn't get placed in service until February 2026 still loses the deduction for the year that actually needed it.

There's a second layer worth planning around too. One hundred percent bonus depreciation isn't always the right move even when you qualify. If you're a growing contracting business projecting a much higher income year in 2026 or 2027, taking the full deduction now might waste it against income that was already low, when spreading depreciation, or electing out of bonus depreciation for a specific asset class, would offset higher future tax brackets more effectively. This is exactly the kind of decision that belongs in a projection conversation before year-end, not a guess made at the equipment dealer's counter.

## Section 179 versus bonus depreciation for contractors

Section 179 and bonus depreciation both let you deduct the full cost of qualifying equipment in the year it's placed in service, but they work differently, and the differences matter for how you sequence purchases.

| | Section 179 | Bonus depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| 2025 limit | $2,500,000 | No dollar cap |
| Phase-out | Begins once total equipment purchases exceed $4,000,000 | None |
| Taxable income limit | Deduction can't exceed business taxable income | No income limitation |
| New or used property | Both qualify | Both qualify |
| Carryforward | Unused deduction carries forward | Not applicable, no income limit to hit |

For most St. George contractors, the taxable income limitation on Section 179 is the detail that trips people up. If your business shows a loss or thin profit in a slow year, a large Section 179 election can't create or deepen that loss the way bonus depreciation can. Bonus depreciation has no income ceiling, which makes it the more flexible tool when you're buying heavy equipment in a year where net income is uncertain until the books close.

Vehicle rules add another wrinkle. Pickup trucks and work trucks with a gross vehicle weight rating over 6,000 pounds, which describes most contractor trucks and heavy-duty crew cabs, qualify for both Section 179 and bonus depreciation without the passenger-vehicle luxury caps that apply to lighter vehicles. If your fleet plan includes a new dump truck or a loaded crew cab, that weight threshold is worth confirming with your CPA before you sign the purchase order, not after.

## The 179D deduction for contractors building energy efficient commercial space

Most equipment-focused tax planning stops at trucks and machinery, but contractors doing commercial design-build work have another deduction worth knowing about. The [Energy Efficient Commercial Buildings Deduction](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-commercial-buildings-deduction), known as Section 179D, rewards efficiency improvements to lighting, HVAC, and building envelope systems in commercial buildings.

This one matters most for general contractors and design-build firms working on government-owned or tax-exempt buildings, such as school districts, municipal buildings, or public facilities anywhere in Washington County. Because tax-exempt entities can't use a deduction themselves, the building owner can allocate the 179D deduction to the designer or contractor responsible for the energy-efficient systems. If your firm has bid public work involving lighting retrofits, upgraded HVAC, or building envelope improvements, it's worth confirming whether that allocation was ever claimed. It's frequently left on the table simply because nobody asked.

## Buy, finance, or lease changes your cash flow math

A deduction is only useful if it lines up with cash you actually have. Contractors on cash-basis accounting sometimes buy equipment specifically to reduce a tax bill, without checking whether the purchase drains working capital right when payroll or a subcontractor payment is due.

Financing the purchase, rather than paying cash, often solves this. You can still take the full Section 179 or bonus depreciation deduction on financed equipment, even though you're only making a down payment and monthly note, which means you get the full tax benefit in the current year while spreading the actual cash outlay over 36 or 60 months. Leasing is a different animal entirely: true operating leases don't generate a depreciation deduction at all, since you don't own the asset, though the lease payment itself is deductible as an ordinary expense. For a contractor deciding between a $180,000 excavator purchase and a lease, the right answer depends on projected income, existing debt service, and how long you plan to keep the machine, not just which option has the lower monthly payment.

This is where entity structure comes into play too. Contractors running multiple entities, an operating company and an equipment-holding company, for example, need the purchase and the placed-in-service documentation to sit with the entity that actually generates the income you're trying to offset. Get that mismatched, and the deduction can end up stranded in an entity with no income to absorb it.

## A Q4 checklist built around your actual numbers

Waiting until the last week of December to think about equipment is how contractors end up buying the wrong thing at the wrong time for the wrong reason. A better sequence looks like this:

- **Run a real income projection by mid-October.** You need to know your actual taxable income trajectory before deciding how much deduction you need, not guess based on last year's return.
- **Confirm delivery and placed-in-service timing with the dealer in writing.** Verbal promises about "before year-end" delivery are not documentation the IRS will accept if the equipment arrives late.
- **Photograph and log the in-service date.** A dated photo of the machine on your jobsite, plus the operator log showing first use, is simple proof that holds up if the deduction is ever questioned.
- **Decide on financing structure before the purchase, not after.** Once the note is signed, restructuring for cash flow reasons gets harder.
- **Match the purchase to the right entity.** If you operate under a WIP schedule and percentage-of-completion accounting, your [WIP schedule](/industries/construction/wip-schedules-percentage-completion) already reflects which entity is carrying the income; the equipment deduction should follow it.

## Common mistakes we see every December

A few patterns show up in almost every contractor's file when equipment planning happens too late:

- Buying equipment based on a tax bill estimate that was never actually run against year-to-date books.
- Taking full bonus depreciation in a low-income year, then having nothing left to offset a much bigger income year that follows.
- Purchasing under a personal name or the wrong entity, then trying to fix the ownership after the fact.
- Assuming a deposit or purchase order counts as "placed in service," then losing the deduction when delivery slips past December 31.
- Skipping the projection conversation entirely and finding out about the deduction limits during tax prep, when the only options left are the ones already locked in.

Every one of these is fixable with a conversation in October or November. None of them are fixable in April.

## Common Questions

**Does a deposit or down payment on equipment count as placed in service?**
No. A deposit only secures the purchase; the deduction depends on the equipment being delivered, assembled, and ready for its intended use in your business. If a truck or excavator doesn't reach that point until January, the deduction moves to next year regardless of when you paid for it.

**Is bonus depreciation still worth using now that it's back to 100 percent?**
Usually yes, but not automatically. If your income is unusually low this year and expected to jump significantly next year, taking 100 percent bonus depreciation now can waste the deduction against income that didn't need it. A projection comparing this year and next is the only way to know for sure.

**Can I still use Section 179 if my business had a loss this year?**
Section 179 can't create or increase a business loss, since it's capped at your taxable income for the year. Bonus depreciation has no such limit, so it's often the better tool in a year where profit is thin or uncertain.

**What documentation do I need to prove an equipment purchase was placed in service by December 31?**
A dated delivery receipt, a photo of the equipment on the jobsite, and an operator or usage log showing first use are the strongest combination. Verbal assurances from a dealer about delivery timing are not enough if the IRS ever asks for proof.
